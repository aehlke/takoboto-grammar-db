### Comment 15 — Radish

Yes, muki's broadest meaning applies to describing an inherent characteristic of something. This grammar entry does a bit of a poor job in elucidating that.<br/><br/>So, sometimes muki translates into "is (inherently) suitable for". <br/><br/>In your example, about summer. The comment is based on an observation of the architecture.Maybe the walls of the cottage are thin. Maybe there is no fireplace, or even electricity, etc.<br/><br/>I could also say:<br/><br/>彼女はアニメの声優向きの声をしている。<br/>She has a voice that's meant for (voice) acting.<br/><br/>In other words, that quality of being meant for voice acting, is inherent the timbre or w/e of her voice.

### Comment 16 — Radish

It is not entirely accurate, but let me share my way of thinking about muke vs muki. <br/><br/>Muke describes the "intent" of something. <br/><br/>So, "X is made for kids." --- but that does not necessarily mean kids will like it.It is someone's intent or belief that this is true.　And maybe it is true, but that is not what is being stressed.<br/><br/>muki's, "X is made for kids." means that "It is obvious that this item contains qualities that kids would enjoy." It is not about anyone's belief or intent.<br/><br/>Kind of like taking the transitive vs intransitive aspects of a verb.
