## Meaning

is, am, are da / desu

(NA-adjective) + da / desu

## Notes

### 1 — Rushyne

I-adjective is a Japanese adjective that ends with i in the dictionary form. An NA-adjective is a Japanese adjective that does not end with i. This is a sentence formula of [(I-adjective) + desu]. An I-adjective doesn't use "da" as a sentence ending. An NA-adjective is any Japanese adjective that doesn't end with i. And the sentence formula of [(NA-adjective) + da/desu]. NA-adjective sentence ends in "da" the plain form and "desu" the polite form.

### 2 — Rinji\_HalfElf

<b>desu, how "to be" came "to be"</b><br/>
<br/>
Pronounced as "deehs" with the "u" being whispered, or near slient.<br/>
<br/>
There are two theories of how "Desu" came to be.<br/>
<br/>
In both instances, <b><a href="http://jgram.org/pages/viewOne.php?tagE="></a></b> is a contraction of a verb.<br/>
<br/>
<b>Theory <b><a href="http://jgram.org/pages/viewOne.php?tagE=is,%20am,%20are#">#</a></b></b><br/>
Some believe desu is a contraction of the verb "De arimasu". <br/>
<br/>
As of today<br/>
"De arimasu" is still used in the military.<br/>
"De arimasu" originally came from a dialect of Choshu (now called Yamaguchi).<br/>
Choshu was one of the driving forces of Meiji Restoration. When ground troops were established, "De arimasu" became part of army speak.<br/>
"De arimasu" was not only reserved for the military. People used the expression, because retired veterans used it in their home-towns at the time.<br/>
As of today, "de arimasu" is still used in formal writing.<br/>
<br/>
<b>Theory <b><a href="http://jgram.org/pages/viewOne.php?tagE=is,%20am,%20are#">#</a></b></b><br/>
<b><a href="http://jgram.org/pages/viewOne.php?tagE="></a></b> is the contraction of "de gozaimasu". "de gozaimasu" is very polite and used today in certain phrases, and formal writing. It's considered samurai speak now.<br/>
<br/>
In both cases, after the caste system from the Edo period fell, everyone was made equal. Everyone also went to school, regardless to whether they were a poor merchants daughter, or of a higher class.<br/>
<br/>
At this time, it is said that it was mostly the merchants daughters who started the contraction of the verb because the polite forms were too confusing to remember. Most merchants were very poor, and could not afford special education before this time. This is highly plausible.<br/>
In modern Japan, most children speak in plain speech, until they are taught polite Japanese in Elementary school. The tradition of polite Japanese being taught in school still exists.<br/>
<br/>
Much to educators dismay at the time, the <b><a href="http://jgram.org/pages/viewOne.php?tagE="></a></b> tend did catch on, and it is now used today.<br/>
<br/>
<b>Other variations of <b><a href="http://jgram.org/pages/viewOne.php?tagE="></a></b></b><br/>
Japanese is a continually changing language. Even desu is changing.<br/>
<br/>
っす ssu is used in colloquial speech.<br/>
The copula is subject to dialectal variation throughout Japan, resulting in forms such as や ya (in Kansai) and じゃ ja (in Hiroshima).<br/>
<br/>
ス su is used by the Yakuza as a shorter form of desu. This is typically used to sound a little more polite when speaking with authorities, but not going all out and using desu.
