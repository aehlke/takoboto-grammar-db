## Meaning

(subject marker)

There is a television in the kitchen.

## Notes

### 1 — Rinji\_HalfElf

<b>Uses of が</b><br/>
<br/>
Ga marks what the Japanese call the grammatical subject of the sentence. Think of the subject in the following two ways:<br/>
<br/>
<b>First in neutral descriptions of observable actions or situations.</b><br/>
<br/>
手紙<strong>が</strong>来ました。　てがみ<strong>が</strong>きました。<br/>
The mail came. 　　Tegami <strong>ga</strong> kimashita.<br/>
<br/>
雨<strong>が</strong>ふっています。　　　あめ<strong>が</strong>ふっています。<br/>
Rain is falling.　　Ame <strong>ga</strong> futte imasu.<br/>
<br/>
<b>*</b>However, が cannot be used in the negative form (because, they are in a sense, unobservable). は　is used instead. <br/>
<br/>
手紙<strong>は</strong>来ませんでした。　てがみ<strong>は</strong>きませんでした。<br/>
The mail didn't come. 　　Tegami <strong>ha</strong> kimasen deshita.<br/>
<br/>
雨<strong>は</strong>ふっていません。　　　あめ<strong>は</strong>ふっていません。<br/>
Rain isn't falling.　　Ame <strong>ha</strong> futte imasen.<br/>
<br/>
<b>Second, for special emphasis, to distinguish a particular person or thing from all others.</b><br/>
<br/>
<b>私</b><strong>が</strong>しました。　　<b>わたし</b><strong>が</strong>しました。<br/>
<b>I</b> did it.   <b>Watashi</b> <strong>ga</strong> shimashita.<br/>
<br/>
<b>ハームバーガ</b><strong>が</strong>緑です。 <b>ハームバーガ</b><strong>が</strong>みどりです。<br/>
<b>(The) Hamburger</b> is green. <b>Haamubaaga</b> <strong>ga</strong> midori desu.<br/>
<br/>
<b>Other Uses.<br/>
When and interrogative pronoun is used as the subject of a sentence, が　must be used.</b><br/>
<br/>
<b>誰</b><strong>が,</strong>これをしましたか？　<b>だれ</b><strong>が,</strong>これをしましたか？<br/>
<b>Who</b> did this?  <b>Dare</b> <strong>ga,</strong> kore wo shimashita ka?

### 2 — Rinji\_HalfElf

<b>Other notes</b><br/>
<br/>
<b>*</b>As an abstract and rough approximation, the difference between は and が is a matter of what the focus of the sentence is. <br/>
<br/>
は gives focus to the <b>action</b> of the sentence, i.e., to the verb or adjective.<br/>
<br/>
が gives focus to the <b>subject of the action</b>.<br/>
<br/>
An example of this can be illustrated with two sentences which mean almost the same thing in English.<br/>
<br/>
（あなたは）日本語を話しますか？　（あなたは）にほんごをはなしますか？　<br/>
<b>Do</b> (you) <b>speak</b> Japanese? (anata ha) nihongo wo hanashimasu ka?<br/>
<br/>
（あなたは）日本語が話せますか？　（あなたは）にほんごがはなせますか？　<br/>
Can (you) speak <b>Japanese</b>? (anata ha) <b>nihongo</b> ga hanasemasu ka?<br/>
<br/>
<b>Exhaustive ga</b><br/>
<br/>
<b>*</b>Unlike は, the subject particle が nominates its referent as the sole satisfier of the predicate. This distinction is famously illustrated by the following pair of sentences.<br/>
<br/>
    ジョン<b>は</b>学生です。    ジョン<b>は</b>がくせいです。 <br/>
    John <b>ha</b> gakusei desu<br/>
    John is a student. (There may be other students among the people we're talking about.)<br/>
<br/>
ジョン<b>が</b>学生です。ジョン<b>が</b>がくせいです。 <br/>
    John <b>ga</b> gakusei desu.<br/>
    (Of all the people we are talking about) it is John who is the student. <br/>
<br/>

### 3 — Rinji\_HalfElf

<b>Transitive and Intransitive verbs and が</b><br/>
<br/>
Japanese has a large variety of related pairs of transitive verbs (that take a direct object) and intransitive verbs (that do not take a direct object), such as hajimaru, (an activity) begins and hajimeru, (an actor) begins (an activity). <br/>
<br/>
    授業<strong>が</strong>始まる。 じゅぎょう<strong></strong>はじまる。<br/>
    Jugyou <strong>ga</strong> hajimaru.<br/>
    The <b>class</b> starts<strong>(intransitive)</strong>. (It is the class which does the starting on it's own, therefore it's observable, like rain.)<br/>
<br/>
    先生<strong>が</strong>授業を始める。せんせいが授業を始める。<br/>
    Sensei <strong>ga</strong> jugyou o hajimeru.<br/>
    The <b>teacher</b> starts<strong>(transitive)</strong> the class. (Of all the teachers, this one starts class.)<br/>
<br/>
Then there's another variant:<br/>
   先生<strong>は</strong>授業を始める。せんせいは授業を始める。<br/>
    Sensei <strong>ha</strong> jugyou o hajimeru.<br/>
    The teacher(s) start(s)<strong>(transitive)</strong> the class. (Teachers in general start the class.)<br/>
<br/>
And another variant:<br/>
   先生<strong>のみは</strong>授業を始める。せんせいのみは授業を始める。<br/>
    Sensei nomi<strong>ha</strong> jugyou o hajimeru.<br/>
    The teacher(s) start(s)<strong>(transitive)</strong> the class. (Only teachers in general start the class. Sounds silly, but many times this is understood as a general statement in English without the extra words.)<br/>
<br/>
Yet another example:<br/>
   先生のみ<strong>が</strong>授業を始める。せんせいのみが授業を始める。<br/>
    Sensei nomi<strong>ga</strong> jugyou o hajimeru.<br/>
    The <b>teacher</b> starts<strong>(transitive)</strong> the class. (Out of all the teachers, this one starts the class <b>only</b>.)<br/>
<br/>
And if nomi "のみ" is moved next to the verb as "のみ始める", it might indicate that no one else starts the class but this teacher (and not you).<br/>
<br/>
<br/>
In many cases the subject is omitted. Had the "teacher" been the topic of conversation in the first transitive example, the following would occur:<br/>
<br/>
(先生が) 授業を始める。（せんせいが）授業を始める。<br/>
    （Sensei ga) Jugyou o hajimeru.<br/>
　　　Class starts<strong>(transitive)</strong>. (By an actor already mentioned, as indicated by the transitive verb or previous context）<br/>
<br/>
The omitted subject is also what makes this topic more difficult to explain. Many examples used do not include the omitted subject or previous context which is important to understanding for many learners.<br/>
Those who speak English and begin to learn Japanese see most articles on this subject as a list of grammatical memorizations because they are not proficient with omitted subject. Even if a student knows how to drop a subject, they also must learn to when insert one for some random examples to make sense. Transitive and intransitive makes this a little clearer.

### 9 — Svendsen

You can read Tae Kim's online guide to japanese and get a very reasonable explanation. First of all he rejects the use of the term subject, simply because it is laden with all the wrong connotations from english. Primarily because subject in japanese is not the same as subject in english (and most roman and germanic languages, probably). He calls it the identifier, a particle used to identfy one among many, where as ha is used to say something detailed about something, by some called the focus particle. <br/>
