## Examples

### Example 558

今年こそは修士論文を書き上げて**みせる**。

ことしこそはしゅうしろんぶんをかきあげてみせる。

en: This very year or never, I'm going to finish my MA thesis, **if you will.**

Credits: dc

en: I win by all means.

### Example 3753

### Comment 9 — skottt

This is a tricky one. I think that the translation into "I'll show you" is good - except that you wouldn't really say that in English.<br/>It is a form used when you have something to prove, or the desire to show someone that you can/will do (whatever).<br/>That said, in English there is no set phrase commonly uttered aloud to express that feeling.<br/>(Other than "I'll show you/him/her/them!", but that is more often said to one's self, rather than to another.<br/>Compare <br/>りんごを食べる！　<br/>- I will eat an apple<br/><br/>りんごを食べてみせる！<br/>- I will eat an apple (*while thinking* ...and show you! You said I couldn't, so this will show you how wrong you are!)

### Comment 10 — Televangelist

There are plenty of good translations in English.<br/><br/>The phrases "Just you wait!" and "You'll see!" pop immediately to mind, in terms of phrases you can use when you've got something to prove.<br/><br/>The only difference? It's not in vogue in America to directly convey that you have something to prove -- has a bit of the air of a children's cartoon. <br/><br/>As a result, both of the phrases above, while a good translation, are mostly only used in a silly or archaic fashion in English.<br/><br/>For instance, when I make fun of my girlfriend, she always replies defiantly, "One day I'll show you, you big lout!"<br/><br/>You could translate that to "Itsuka Utte miseru!" (The "utsu" verb for exacting revenge, I forget the kanji)<br/><br/>It's archaic language used to convey a silly effect, not because the words are "old fashioned" but because the *feeling behind them* isn't one that English speakers like to directly express.
